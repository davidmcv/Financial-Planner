🏦 Premium Bonds & NS&I

Held apart from ordinary savings because the return is a prize draw, not interest: it pays nothing towards your Personal Savings Allowance and nothing in tax. The prize rate is an average — a typical holding wins rather less than it.

Each person may hold up to £50,000, and every prize is free of UK income tax and capital gains tax — so a couple can shelter £100,000 between them with nothing to declare and nothing to report on a tax return. That makes them most useful once your ISA allowance is used up and your savings interest would otherwise be taxed. The trade-off is that the return is a lottery rather than a rate: below-average luck on a small holding can mean years with no prizes at all, and the money is not protected against inflation.

Maximum holding £50,000 per person.

On top of your current plan, invested until you retire
Takes your tax-free lump sum (up to £268,275) at your pension access age and invests it in a General Investment Account in your spouse's name, so future gains are taxed at their lower rate.
⚠Think hard before doing this. Taking money out of a pension to put it back into an ISA usually costs more in tax than it saves: 75% of each withdrawal is taxed as income on the way out, while the ISA only shelters what is left. It is worth it mainly if you would otherwise have unused personal allowance, or you want money outside the pension for a specific reason. On inheritance tax the old answer has changed: .

🔄 Keep paying into a pension after you retire

You can carry on paying into a pension after you stop working, and the provider still adds 20% basic-rate relief — up to £3,600 gross a year (£2,880 from your pocket) even with no earnings at all, right up to age 75. Turn it on for either of you and the plan below shows whether it is actually worth doing.

The money leaves your savings and lands in your pension grossed up by the 20% relief. Capped at £2,880 net once you have no earnings, and stops at 75.
Paying into someone else's pension is fully permitted — the relief follows their tax position, not yours, so it works best when they are a non- or basic-rate taxpayer.
